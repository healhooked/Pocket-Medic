**Why are neonates more subject to hypothermia?**
Neonates are more susceptible to hypothermia due to several physiological and anatomical factors. Firstly, they have a **large surface area-to-volume ratio**, which increases heat loss through the skin.[1-2] This is particularly pronounced in preterm infants who have even less subcutaneous fat and thinner skin, further exacerbating heat loss.[1][3]

Secondly, neonates have **immature thermoregulatory mechanisms**. Their ability to generate heat through shivering and non-shivering thermogenesis is not fully developed, making it difficult for them to maintain body temperature in response to environmental changes.[1][4] Additionally, their metabolic rate, although higher than adults, can be quickly overwhelmed, leading to metabolic exhaustion and hypothermia.[2]

Thirdly, the **environmental transition** from the intrauterine to extrauterine life plays a significant role. The intrauterine environment is thermoneutral, and upon birth, neonates are exposed to cooler ambient temperatures, which can lead to rapid heat loss if not managed properly.

**Explain why pregnant women are at a higher risk of pulmonary embolism?**
Pregnant women are at a higher risk of pulmonary embolism (PE) due to several physiological and pathological changes that occur during pregnancy. The increased risk is primarily attributed to the following factors:

1. **Hypercoagulability**: Pregnancy is a hypercoagulable state, which is thought to have evolved to protect against the bleeding challenges of miscarriage and childbirth. This state is characterized by increased levels of coagulation factors (II, VII, VIII, and X), decreased levels of free protein S, and acquired resistance to activated protein C.[1-2]
2. **Venous Stasis**: There is a reduction in venous flow velocity in the legs by approximately 50% by 25 to 29 weeks of gestation, which persists until about 6 weeks postpartum. This stasis is exacerbated by the mechanical compression of the inferior vena cava and pelvic veins by the enlarging uterus, particularly in the third trimester.[1][3]
3. **Vascular Damage**: The process of delivery can cause endothelial damage to the pelvic vessels, further increasing the risk of thrombus formation. This risk is particularly pronounced in the postpartum period.[3-4]
4. **Additional Risk Factors**: Other factors that contribute to the increased risk include inherited and acquired thrombophilias, a history of previous venous thromboembolism (VTE), obesity, immobility, advanced maternal age, and certain medical conditions such as diabetes and lupus.
