- COPD is often associated with emphysema, which involves the destruction of alveolar walls and the formation of bullae (large air spaces). These bullae can rupture, leading to the escape of air into the pleural space and resulting in a pneumothorax. The presence of emphysema significantly increases the risk of pneumothorax, as demonstrated in studies where increased pleural-based emphysema was independently associated with a higher risk of past pneumothorax.

- Secondly, the structural changes in the lungs of COPD patients, such as decreased lung elasticity and increased airway resistance, contribute to the development of pneumothorax. These changes can cause areas of the lung to become overinflated and more susceptible to rupture under stress, such as during coughing or physical exertion.

- Additionally, the compromised lung function in COPD patients, characterized by reduced forced expiratory volume (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC), further predisposes them to pneumothorax. Patients with severe airflow obstruction (FEV1 < 1 liter or FEV1/FVC < 40%) are at the greatest risk.

- In summary, the increased susceptibility to pneumothorax in COPD patients is primarily due to the presence of emphysema, structural lung changes, and compromised lung function, which collectively increase the likelihood of alveolar or bullae rupture.
