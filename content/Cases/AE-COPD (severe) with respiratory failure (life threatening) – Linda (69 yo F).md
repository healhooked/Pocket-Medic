Hypercapnic respiratory failure secondary to excessive oxygen administration is a significant concern in the management of acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD). All COPD patients with moderate to severe exacerbation are considered at risk. The proposed mechanisms include:

1. **Worsening of ventilation-perfusion (VQ) mismatch (most important)**: This is the primary mechanism. Oxygen administration can lead to the release of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, resulting in increased perfusion to poorly ventilated areas of the lung, thereby worsening VQ mismatch and increasing PaCO₂.[1-2]

2. **Inhibition of ventilatory drive**: COPD patients often rely on hypoxic drive to maintain ventilation. High levels of supplemental oxygen can suppress this drive, leading to hypoventilation and subsequent CO₂ retention.[3-4]

3. **The Haldane effect**: Oxygen administration can displace CO₂ from hemoglobin, increasing the amount of dissolved CO₂ in the blood, which contributes to hypercapnia.[2]

4. **Absorption atelectasis**: High concentrations of oxygen can lead to the absorption of alveolar gas, causing alveolar collapse and further impairing gas exchange.[5]

5. **Higher viscosity of oxygen (O₂)**: This is not a recognized mechanism in the medical literature and should be disregarded.

The Lung Foundation Australia recommends targeting an SpO₂ of 88-92% in COPD patients to avoid these complications.[6] The Australian and New Zealand Committee on Resuscitation also emphasizes controlled lower concentration oxygen therapy to prevent hypercapnic respiratory failure.[7]
