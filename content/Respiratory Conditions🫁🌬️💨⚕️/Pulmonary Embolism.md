**The primary reason to hesitate on administering fluids to patients with a pulmonary embolism (PE) is the risk of exacerbating right ventricular (RV) dysfunction.**

In patients with PE, especially those with significant clot burden, the right ventricle faces increased afterload due to the obstruction in the pulmonary arteries. This can lead to RV dilation and dysfunction. Administering large volumes of fluids can increase the preload, which the compromised right ventricle may not be able to handle effectively, potentially worsening RV failure and leading to hemodynamic instability.[1-3]

**Key points include:**
1. **Right Ventricular Dysfunction**: The right ventricle is already under strain due to the increased afterload from the pulmonary embolism. Excessive fluid administration can exacerbate this strain, leading to further RV dilation and decreased cardiac output.[1-2]

2. **Hemodynamic Instability**: In patients with PE, particularly those with submassive or massive PE, the right ventricle's ability to handle increased volumes is compromised. This can lead to a cycle of worsening RV function and systemic hypotension.[1-3]

3. **Clinical Evidence**: Studies have shown that fluid administration in patients with submassive PE is associated with adverse outcomes. For instance, a retrospective chart review indicated an incremental increase in adverse events with each liter of fluid administered (odds ratio 2.1, 95% confidence interval, 1.4-3.2).[3]

4. **Guideline Recommendations**: The American Heart Association and other expert guidelines recommend cautious use of fluids in the management of PE, emphasizing the need to avoid fluid overload and to consider alternative supportive measures such as vasopressors if needed.[1-2][4]

In summary, while fluid administration is a common initial response in many forms of shock, in the context of PE, it must be approached with caution due to the potential for worsening RV dysfunction and hemodynamic instability.
