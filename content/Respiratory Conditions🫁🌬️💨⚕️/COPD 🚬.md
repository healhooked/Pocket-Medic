# Chronic bronchitis (airway-dominant disease)
Chronic irritation leads to:
- Mucus gland hypertrophy
- Goblet cell hyperplasia
- Excessive mucus production → airway plugging

Thickening of airway walls from inflammation, fibrosis & remodelling.

The net effect is narrowed airways with increased resistance.

Clinically, this is the “productive cough” phenotype. Gas exchange becomes inefficient because ventilation is uneven—some alveoli are perfused but poorly ventilated (low V/Q ratio), contributing to hypoxia and, later, hypercapnia.

# Emphysema (parenchymal destruction)
Here the key mechanism is a protease–antiprotease imbalance (e.g., excess elastase activity).
This leads to:
- Destruction of alveolar walls
- Loss of elastic recoil

Collapse of small airways during expiration

This causes air trapping and hyperinflation. The lungs become mechanically inefficient—patients must work harder to exhale. Surface area for diffusion drops, so oxygen transfer is impaired even if air reaches the alveoli.

Physiology:
Airflow limitation: Due to airway narrowing + loss of elastic recoil → especially affects expiration → prolonged expiratory phase
Air trapping & hyperinflation: Increases residual volume → flattens diaphragm → worsens work of breathing
Gas exchange abnormalities:
Early: hypoxia from V/Q mismatch
Late: hypercapnia as ventilation fails
Pulmonary hypertension: Chronic hypoxic vasoconstriction → vascular remodeling → can progress to right heart failure (cor pulmonale)
There’s also a systems-level perspective. COPD reflects a failure of regulatory balance:
Inflammation vs repair
Protease vs antiprotease
Oxidative stress vs antioxidant defenses

# Cor Pulmonale

- Rightward deviation of the P wave and QRS axis
- Low voltage QRS complexes, especially in the left precordial leads (V4-6)
- With development of cor pulmonale, right atrial enlargement (P pulmonale) and [[Right Ventricular Hypertrophy (RVH)]]
- Arrhythmias including multifocal atrial tachycardia

Hypercapnic respiratory failure secondary to excessive oxygen administration is a significant concern in the management of acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD). All COPD patients with moderate to severe exacerbation are considered at risk. The proposed mechanisms include:

1. **Worsening of ventilation-perfusion (VQ) mismatch (most important)**: This is the primary mechanism. Oxygen administration can lead to the release of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, resulting in increased perfusion to poorly ventilated areas of the lung, thereby worsening VQ mismatch and increasing PaCO₂.[1-2]

2. **Inhibition of ventilatory drive**: COPD patients often rely on hypoxic drive to maintain ventilation. High levels of supplemental oxygen can suppress this drive, leading to hypoventilation and subsequent CO₂ retention.[3-4]

3. **The Haldane effect**: Oxygen administration can displace CO₂ from hemoglobin, increasing the amount of dissolved CO₂ in the blood, which contributes to hypercapnia.[2]

4. **Absorption atelectasis**: High concentrations of oxygen can lead to the absorption of alveolar gas, causing alveolar collapse and further impairing gas exchange.[5]

5. **Higher viscosity of oxygen (O₂)**: This is not a recognized mechanism in the medical literature and should be disregarded.

The Lung Foundation Australia recommends targeting an SpO₂ of 88-92% in COPD patients to avoid these complications.[6] The Australian and New Zealand Committee on Resuscitation also emphasizes controlled lower concentration oxygen therapy to prevent hypercapnic respiratory failure.[7]
