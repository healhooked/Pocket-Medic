**Clinical Features**
- Ubilical cord visible at, or external to, the vaginal opening. 
- Evidence of membranes having ruptured. 
- Change in fetal movement pattern. 
- Meconium in the amniotic fluid (vaginal discharge may be stained green)

This condition worsens as labour progresses and the presenting part descends, compressing the cord and cutting off the fetal blood supply, leading to hypoxia and eventual fetal demise.

**Risk Factors**
- Malpresentation (breech, oblique, transverse or unstable lie) 
- Pre-term gestational age 
- Low birth weight 
- Second twin 
- Low lying placentation 
- Pelvic deformities 
- Uterine malformations/tumors 
- External fetal abnormalities 
- Polyhydramnios 
- Long umbilical card 
- Unengaged presenting part 
- Prolonged labour 
- Atypical placental cord insertions (velamentous and marginal)

Caesarean section is the recommended mode of delivery in cases of cord prolapse when vaginal birth is not imminent in order to prevent fetal hypoxic acidosis.
• Cord presentation or prolapse should be excluded by visual vaginal examination in labour after spontaneous rupture of membranes (ask mother to feel for the cord).
• Caution is required if manoeuvring the umbilical cord as pinching can cause vasospasm, use a DRY pad to replace the cord within the opening of the vulva and prevent further prolapse (underwear may be used to hold pad in place)
