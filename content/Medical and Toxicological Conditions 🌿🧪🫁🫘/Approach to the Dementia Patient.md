🔹 5. Consider Delirium vs. Dementia

Feature	Delirium	Dementia

Onset	Sudden (hours–days)	Gradual (months–years)
Course	Fluctuates	Steady decline
Attention	Impaired	Often preserved
Consciousness	May be altered	Normal until late
Cause	Often reversible (infection, drugs)	Progressive brain disease


If a known dementia patient suddenly worsens, think delirium until proven otherwise (e.g., UTI, sepsis, dehydration, hypoxia, polypharmacy).

🔹 6. Use of Prehospital Tools

No gold-standard dementia-specific tool is validated for prehospital use, but you can use:

✅ ISBAR or IMIST-AMBO Handover:

Include dementia status in the “Assessment” or “Medical History” component:

“Pt is known to have moderate Alzheimer’s. Baseline is forgetful but ambulant and communicative. Today is acutely confused with no known precipitant.”


✅ 6-Item Screener (Modified for quick use):

Year?

Month?

Age?

Repeat 3 objects (e.g., ball, tree, book)
Count backward from 20 to 1
Recall the 3 items
Score ≤4 suggests cognitive impairment. Takes under 1 minute.


**[[Approach to the Dementia Patient]]**
- 🧠 **Rapid Screening**: Use A&Ox4 and 3-word recall to detect delirium, confusion, or cognitive decline; 6-item screener helps flag impairment in under a minute.
- 👥 **Collateral + Functional Clues**: Get history from carers; assess baseline, changes, and function (e.g. mobility, communication, meds).
- 🔄 **Delirium vs Dementia**: Sudden fluctuation = delirium (often reversible); gradual decline = dementia (progressive). Always rule out delirium first.
