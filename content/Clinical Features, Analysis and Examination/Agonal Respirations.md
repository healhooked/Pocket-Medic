**Agonal respirations** are abnormal, irregular, and often slow gasping breaths that occur in the setting of severe cerebral hypoxia, most commonly during cardiac arrest or near-death states. These respirations are characterized by their ineffectiveness for adequate ventilation and oxygenation, and are not compatible with sustained life. The American Heart Association describes agonal breathing as slow, irregular gasping respirations that are frequently mistaken by laypersons as signs of life, which can delay recognition of cardiac arrest and initiation of CPR.[1]

Agonal respirations originate from lower brainstem centers as higher cortical regions become hypoxic, and are a universal phenomenon in mammals following anoxia or ischemia.[2-3] They are present in up to 40–60% of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest cases and are associated with witnessed events and ventricular fibrillation.[1][3-4] While agonal respirations may produce some degree of ventilation and transiently support circulation, they are not sufficient for survival and should be recognized as a sign of cardiac arrest requiring immediate resuscitative efforts.[1][3][5]

Clinically, agonal respirations are described by bystanders with terms such as "gasping," "snoring," or "abnormal breathing," and their presence should prompt rapid initiation of CPR in unresponsive patients.[1][5-7] Recognition and education about agonal breathing are critical for improving cardiac arrest outcomes, as their presence is associated with increased survival when CPR is promptly initiated.[1][4-5]
