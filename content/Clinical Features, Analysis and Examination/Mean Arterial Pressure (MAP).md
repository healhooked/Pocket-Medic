Mean arterial pressure (MAP) is a proxy for organ perfusion pressure. 

Clinically, it answers one question: is blood actually reaching tissues at a level sufficient to sustain aerobic metabolism?

Core concept MAP reflects the balance between cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance. In practical terms, it approximates the pressure driving blood through capillary beds.

MAP \approx DBP + \frac{1}{3}(SBP - DBP)

That formula matters less than what it represents physiologically: diastole dominates time, so MAP is weighted toward DBP.

# High-yield clinical thresholds

- < 65 mmHg
This is the widely accepted lower limit for adequate perfusion in most adults. Below this:

Renal blood flow drops → acute kidney injury risk
Cerebral perfusion becomes pressure-dependent
Lactate rises (shift toward anaerobic metabolism)

- 65–70 mmHg
Standard resuscitation target in shock (especially septic shock)

> 75–85 mmHg
May be required in:

Chronic hypertension (shifted autoregulation curve)

Traumatic brain injury (to maintain cerebral perfusion pressure)




---

# Organ-specific implications

Brain
Cerebral autoregulation normally maintains constant flow between MAP ~60–150 mmHg. Outside this:

Low MAP → ischemia → altered LOC

High MAP → risk of edema or hemorrhage


Kidneys
Highly pressure-sensitive:

MAP <65 → reduced GFR → oliguria

Sustained hypoperfusion → acute tubular necrosis


Heart (coronary perfusion)
Occurs mainly during diastole:

Low MAP (especially low DBP) → myocardial ischemia, even without coronary disease



---

# Shock states (pattern recognition)

Hypovolaemic shock: low MAP due to ↓ preload → ↓ CO

Cardiogenic shock: low MAP despite high SVR → pump failure

Distributive shock (e.g. sepsis): low MAP due to ↓ SVR despite normal/high CO

Obstructive shock: low MAP from impaired cardiac filling/output (PE, tamponade)


MAP helps you differentiate mechanism, not just detect hypotension.


---

Prehospital / ED relevance

Guides vasopressor initiation (e.g. noradrenaline targeting MAP ≥65)

More reliable than systolic BP alone in shock states

Useful in sedation and RSI: post-induction hypotension = drop in MAP → cerebral/coronary hypoperfusion

In trauma: permissive hypotension vs maintaining MAP depends on bleeding vs brain injury priorities



---

Subtle but important points

A “normal” BP (e.g. 100/60) can still give a borderline MAP (~73) — acceptable in some, inadequate in others

Elderly or chronically hypertensive patients often need higher MAPs to perfuse organs

MAP is a lagging indicator—by the time it drops, compensatory mechanisms are already failing
