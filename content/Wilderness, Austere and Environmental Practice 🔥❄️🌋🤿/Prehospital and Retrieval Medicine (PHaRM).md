# **MODULE 1 — INTRODUCTION TO PHARM**

## **1.1 Overview of PHaRM**

**PHaRM = delivery of medical/nursing/paramedic care + safe transport** from scene or facility to definitive care.

### **Key Drivers of Demand**

- ↑ Population growth & ageing
- Rural/remote population distribution
- Deskilling of regional workforce (post‑COVID)
- Centralisation of specialist services
- Increased adventure sports
- ↑ Ambulance interfacility transfer demand + ICU bed shortages

### **Two Primary Questions**

1. **Does PHARM improve patient outcomes?**
    - Complex to measure—affected by transport mode, altitude physiology, tasking bias, patient severity.
2. **How does PHARM affect health systems & cost?**
    - Includes mission cost, system‑level costs, opportunity costs, and resource displacement.

### **Key Concepts**

- **Safety vs benefit trade‑off** (risk management)
- **Patient selection bias** affects outcome analysis
- **Different models ≠ comparable outcomes** (fixed wing vs rotary, paramedic vs doctor crews)

## **Common Terminology (High Yield)**

- **Primary retrieval / scene response** — direct to scene
- **Modified primary** — in basic clinic/aid post
- **Secondary retrieval / IHT** — facility → higher‑level facility
- **Tertiary retrieval** — long‑distance, usually international
- **HEMS** — Helicopter Emergency Medical Service
- **SAR** — Search & rescue (patient not yet located)
- **Challenge–response** — two‑person verification
- **SitRep** — situation report
- **Scoop and run** — fast transport, minimal intervention
- **Stay and play** — maximal on‑scene care

# **MODULE 1.2 — HISTORY OF AEROMEDICAL RETRIEVAL**

### **Military Origins**

- WW1: first fixed‑wing evacuations
- WW2: first rotary use (SE Asia)
- **Korean War:** routine AME missions
- **Vietnam War:** treatment provided _during_ flight → modern critical care retrieval
- Mortality improved due to ↓ time to surgery + highly trained teams

### **Australia**

- **RFDS founded 1928** → world’s longest‑standing aeromedical service
- 1970s–80s: expansion of helicopter services (Victoria 1971, NSW Westpac 1973)
- 1990s–2000s: growth of coordinated, critical‑care capable retrieval systems

### **International**

- 1972 Denver: first hospital‑based HEMS (US)
- Now >700 air ambulance services in the US
- European models emphasise **physician-led HEMS**

### **Modern Civilian Advances**

- ICU‑level care in aircraft
- CCATT / MERT military systems influencing civilian practice
- Doctor‑paramedic (rotary) and doctor‑nurse (fixed wing) models across Australia
- Universal winch rescue capability for most rotary services

# **MODULE 1.3 — RETRIEVAL SYSTEMS (AUSTRALIA + INTERNATIONAL)**

## **Australian Systems**

Key themes:

- Mixed funding (state, NGO, RFDS)
- Centralised **clinical coordination**
- Doctor–paramedic (helicopter), doctor–nurse (fixed wing)
- Paediatric/neonatal dedicated services

### **State Snapshots**

**QLD:**

- Retrieval Services Queensland (RSQ) centralised coordination
- LifeFlight, QGAir, RFDS → integrated system
- CHQRS paediatric retrievals

**SA:**

- **MedSTAR** single statewide service
- Adult + paediatric + neonatal under one umbrella
- 40% rotary, 30% fixed wing, 30% road

**NSW:**

- Centralised AOC
- GS HEMS (doctor–paramedic) + NGO operators
- RFDS Dubbo + Broken Hill

**WA:**

- RFDS primary provider
- Transitioning coordination system
- Jet capability for remote islands

**VIC:**

- Adult Retrieval Victoria (ARV)
- PIPER (paeds/neonates)

**NT:**

- CareFlight Top End
- MRaCC in Alice Springs for remote advice + coordination

## **International Models**

**Anglo‑American** → “take the patient to hospital”

- Paramedic‑led, rapid transport focus

**Franco‑German** → “bring hospital to patient”

- Physician‑led, extensive scene care

➡️ Real‑world practice = blended models; debate is largely outdated.

# **MODULE 2 — ALTITUDE PHYSIOLOGY**

## **2.1 Gas Laws (Critical for exam)**

### **Boyle’s Law (P↓ → Volume↑)**

Applied risks:

- Pneumothorax → tension
- Bowel gas expansion → obstruction worsens
- ETT cuff overinflation
- Air splints / IV drip chambers expand

**Intervention:** vent chest, use saline ETT cuffs, consider cabin altitude restriction.

---

### **Dalton’s Law (↓ total pressure → ↓ partial pressure of O₂)**

At altitude:

- ↓ PaO₂ → ↑ risk of hypoxia
- Early signs: tachycardia, tachypnoea
- Later: confusion, LOC, death

**Mitigation:**

- Supplemental O₂
- Pressurised cabin
- Avoid high altitude for hypoxic patients

---

### **Charles’ Law (Volume ∝ Temperature)**

- Ventilated gases cool/contract at altitude
- Paediatric lungs are more vulnerable
- Monitor airway pressures + gas temperature

---

### **Henry’s Law (↓ pressure → gas comes out of solution)**

Applied risks:

- Decompression illness (divers)
- Nitrogen bubble formation
- Exacerbation of gas‑containing pathology

**Do NOT fly DCI patients unless in pressurised aircraft at sea‑level cabin pressure.**

---

# **2.2 STRESSES OF FLIGHT — GHOSTBAN**

**G – G forces** (acceleration/deceleration)  
**H – Humidity** (dry cabin → dehydration)  
**O – Oxygen** (hypoxia risk with altitude)  
**S – Space** (restricted cabin → limited manoeuvring)  
**T – Temperature** (ambient ↓ with altitude → hypothermia)  
**B – Barometric pressure** (Boyle’s Law implications)  
**A – Acceleration** (affects organ movement & patient stability)  
**N – Noise** (communication failure, fatigue, missed alarms)

---

# **2.3 Applied Flight Physiology**

**Who deteriorates at altitude?**

- DCI patients
- COPD/respiratory failure
- Bowel obstruction
- Pneumothorax
- New dental work
- Recent neurosurgery with intracranial air

**Key Applied Principles**

- Decompress pneumothorax preflight
- Consider ground transport if bowel obstruction
- Maintain neonatal thermal control (transport incubator)
- Manage cabin altitude (target sea‑level)
- Monitor temperature, vibration effects, noise interference

# **MODULE 3 — RETRIEVAL COORDINATION**

## **Core Principle**

**“Right patient, right place, right time, right team, right transport.”**

### **Role of Clinical Coordination**

- Triage mission requests
- Match clinical need to team skill
- Determine transport platform
- Maintain situational awareness
- Manage competing mission demands
- Integrate telehealth
- Balance clinical vs aviation risk
- Support local clinicians

### **Key Decision Factors**

- Patient time‑criticality
- Capability of referring/receiving centres
- Crew availability & skill mix
- Weather, terrain, distance
- Aircraft performance & duty limits
- Community impact of asset removal
- Opportunity cost

### **Tasking Models**

- **Clinician-led:** best accuracy, nuanced decision-making
- **Dispatcher-led:** efficient but risk over/under-triage
- **Hybrid:** increasingly common

➡️ Evidence strongly supports embedded **clinician involvement** in dispatch.

### **Scoring Systems**

- Useful conceptually but **should not replace senior judgement**
- Injury severity tools have poor prehospital predictive reliability

---

# **Telehealth in Retrieval**

Uses:

- Remote assessment
- Procedure guidance (e.g. FONA, thoracostomy)
- Prioritisation decisions
- Post‑consult avoidance of unnecessary retrievals

Limitations:

- Connectivity
- Local resource variation
- Requires senior clinician oversight

---

# **SAR & Disaster Coordination**

- Multiagency (Police, Fire, AMSA, SES, health)
- SAR missions carry high aviation risk → require justification
- PHARM team may be diverted if medical mission arises
- Single coordination point essential during disasters
- Awareness of asset capability → winch, night ops, fuel, location

---

# **THEMES THROUGHOUT ALL MODULES**

- **Risk vs benefit** drives all decision-making
- **Transport critical vs treatment critical**
- **Rural/remote inequity** drives PHARM demand
- **Coordination must be centralised, clinically led**
- **Tasking decisions affect the whole system, not just one patient**
