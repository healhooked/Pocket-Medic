[[Diagnostic Criteria]]
[[DSM- 5]]
PPD is classified as **a major depressive episode with peripartum onset**, meaning it occurs **during pregnancy or within four weeks after delivery**.
#### **[[Diagnostic Criteria]] for Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) with Peripartum Onset:**

At least **five** of the following symptoms must be present for **at least two weeks**, including either **(1) depressed mood** or **(2) loss of interest or pleasure**:

- **Depressed mood** most of the day, nearly every day
- **Markedly diminished interest or pleasure** in most activities
- **Significant weight loss or gain**, or change in appetite
- **Insomnia or excessive sleep (hypersomnia)**
- **Psychomotor agitation or retardation**
- **Fatigue or loss of energy**
- **Feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt**
- **Diminished ability to think or concentrate, or indecisiveness**
- **Recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation, or a suicide attempt**

Additionally:

- The symptoms cause significant distress or impairment in daily functioning.
- The symptoms are **not due to substance use, medical conditions, or normal postpartum changes**.

The DSM-5-TR also recognizes **postpartum [[Psychosis Disorders]]** as a rare but severe variant of PPD, often associated with **bipolar disorder**.

### ICD-11

ICD-11 classifies PPD under **"Depressive episode"** but allows it to be **specified as occurring in the postpartum period**, defined as **within six weeks of childbirth**.

The [[diagnostic criteria]] for a depressive episode in ICD-11 are similar to DSM-5-TR, requiring:

- **At least two core symptoms**:
    - Depressed mood
    - Loss of interest/pleasure
    - Increased fatigability
- **Plus at least two additional symptoms**, including:
    - Sleep disturbances
    - Appetite changes
    - Guilt/worthlessness
    - Suicidal thoughts

Unlike DSM-5, ICD-11 uses a **six-week** postpartum period rather than four weeks.

### **Key Differences Between DSM-5-TR and ICD-11:**

|Feature|DSM-5-TR|ICD-11|
|---|---|---|
|Onset Period|During pregnancy or **first 4 weeks postpartum**|Up to **6 weeks postpartum**|
|Classification|Subtype of **Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)**|**Depressive episode, postpartum specified**|
|Number of Symptoms|**5+ symptoms** (at least one core)|**4+ symptoms** (at least two core)|
**Strengths:* of DSM

- Standardized criteria for clinical diagnosis
- Recognized by psychiatrists, psychologists, and healthcare professionals
- Used for research, insurance, and treatment planning

❌ **Limitations:** of DSM

- The **four-week postpartum timeframe** is outdated; many experts and studies suggest **PPD can develop anytime within the first year**.
- **Lacks emphasis on hormonal, social, and biological factors** that contribute to PPD.
- **Does not differentiate PPD from other types of depression**, despite unique postpartum-specific symptoms.
### **Screening Tools for PPD**

Clinicians often use standardized tools for screening, such as:

- **Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS)** – Most commonly used, a score **≥10–13** suggests PPD risk.
- **Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)** – General depression screening tool.
- **Postpartum Depression Screening Scale (PDSS)** – More specific for postpartum depression symptoms.

### - Risk factors

Psychiatric and Psychological Risk Factors:
- **O'Hara & McCabe (2013)**: _Postpartum depression: Current status and future directions_ (Annual Review of Clinical Psychology) – Reviews **history of depression, anxiety, and trauma** as major contributors to postpartum depression.
- **Robertson et al. (2004)**: _Risk factors for postpartum depression: A meta-[[analysis]]_ (Archives of Women’s Mental Health) – Confirms **stress, self-esteem, and personal coping styles** as predictive factors.

Biological and Hormonal:
- **Bloch et al. (2003)**: _The role of estrogen and progesterone in postpartum depression_ (JAMA Psychiatry) – This study highlights the **sharp hormonal fluctuations** after childbirth as a major trigger for mood disorders.
- **Guintivano et al. (2018)**: _Genetic risk factors for postpartum depression_ (Molecular Psychiatry) – Genetic predisposition plays a role in susceptibility to PPD.

Social and Environmental
- **Beck (2001)**: _Predictors of postpartum depression: An update_ (Nursing Research) – Identifies **lack of social support, marital dissatisfaction, and financial stress** as leading social risk factors.
- **Yim et al. (2015)**: _Biopsychosocial risk factors for postpartum depression_ (Nature Reviews Neuroscience) – A comprehensive review integrating **biological, psychological, and social risks**.

Obstetric and Medical
- **Kettunen et al. (2014)**: _Pregnancy and delivery complications as risk factors for postpartum depression_ (Journal of Psychosomatic Obstetrics & Gynecology) – Discusses **C-sections, birth trauma, and premature birth** as triggers for PPD.

Personal and lifestyle
- **Field (2018)**: _Postpartum depression effects, predictors, and interventions_ (Acta Paediatrica) – Explores **sleep deprivation, physical activity, and nutrition** as modifiable lifestyle risk factors.

**DNA Methylation and Hormonal Regulation:** A study identified specific DNA methylation patterns associated with PPD that also predict other hormonal mood disorders, such as premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD). These epigenetic markers are linked to variations in hormones like pregnenolone and allopregnanolone, which are crucial for mood modulation.

### ·       Clinical presentation(s)
__________

**Presentation**
- **Persistent Low Mood** – Ongoing sadness, emptiness, or hopelessness.
- **Anhedonia** – Loss of interest or pleasure in activities, including bonding with the baby.
- **Fatigue & Low Energy** – Extreme exhaustion beyond typical postpartum tiredness.
- **Anxiety & Irritability** – Excessive worry, agitation, or feeling overwhelmed.
- **Guilt & Feelings of Inadequacy** – Self-doubt, shame, or feeling like a bad mother.
______________

At least **one symptom must be** either **depressed mood** or **loss of interest/pleasure (anhedonia).**

- **Core symptoms of MDD**, including:
    
    - Depressed mood (e.g., persistent sadness, tearfulness)
    - Anhedonia (loss of interest or pleasure in activities)
    - Fatigue or loss of energy
    - Feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt
    - Sleep disturbances (insomnia or hypersomnia)
    - Appetite changes (loss or increase in appetite)
    - Difficulty concentrating or indecisiveness
    - Psychomotor agitation or retardation
    - Suicidal ideation or thoughts of harming oneself or the baby
- **Specific peripartum features:**
    
    - **Severe anxiety** (often mixed with depression)
    - **Obsessive thoughts** about harming the baby (without intent)
    - **Irritability or anger** (towards the baby, partner, or self)
    - **Psychotic symptoms** (rare but serious, such as delusions or hallucinations)
    - **Guilt and inadequacy in mothering abilities**
- **Timing**
    
    - The DSM-5-TR limits the "peripartum onset" specifier to **within four weeks postpartum**, but clinical research suggests that postpartum depression can occur **up to 12 months after birth**.

**Literature:
****1. Predictive Modeling of PPD Risk**

A study developed and validated machine learning models using biopsychosocial predictors—including mental health history, demographics, psychosocial, and physiological factors—to predict PPD risk among perinatal women. Data from 1,138 participants were analyzed between August 2021 and August 2022. ​[translational-medicine.biomedcentral.com](https://translational-medicine.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12967-025-06289-6)

**2. Preventive Strategies Using Mobile Technology**

Researchers assessed the impact of education focused on health-promoting behaviors delivered through social messaging to prevent PPD among pregnant women. The study highlights the potential of app-based interventions to enhance maternal mental health. ​[bmcpsychology.biomedcentral.com+1bmcpregnancychildbirth.biomedcentral.com+1](https://bmcpsychology.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s40359-025-02547-w)

**3. Neurological Changes Associated with PPD**

Groundbreaking research identified a connection between PPD and structural changes in the [[Hippocampus]] and amygdala during pregnancy. This study provides the first evidence of how these brain structures transform in relation to childbirth experiences and perinatal depression symptoms. ​[daijiworld.com](https://www.daijiworld.com/news/newsDisplay?newsID=1274480)

**4. Long-Term Health Implications of PPD**

A prospective cohort study examined the association of PPD with chronic diseases and multimorbidity in women's mid-late life, highlighting the long-term health implications of postpartum depression. ​[bmcmedicine.biomedcentral.com](https://bmcmedicine.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12916-025-03853-1)

**5. Intervention Strategies to Prevent Mental Health Issues**

A scoping review explored the effectiveness of intervention strategies targeting working pregnant women and their partners to prevent mental health problems and improve resilience from conception until the child is 5 years old. ​[bmcpregnancychildbirth.biomedcentral.com](https://bmcpregnancychildbirth.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12884-024-07043-4)
