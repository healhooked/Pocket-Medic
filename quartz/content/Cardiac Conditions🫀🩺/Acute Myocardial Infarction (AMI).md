 **Acute myocardial infarction (acute MI, AMI)** is defined as a condition that is present when **necrosis** (cellular death) of the myocardium occurs because of **prolonged and complete interruption of blood flow** to the area [1, 2]. Myocardial necrosis refers to the irreversible damage to myocardial cells causing their death, which is the result of prolonged lack of [[Oxygen]] [2].

Myocardial ischemia (insufficient [[Oxygen]] supply to meet demand) and injury (severe or prolonged ischemia where cells stop functioning normally) are reversible, but myocardial infarction is not; necrotic cells do not return to normal upon revascularization or oxygenation [3-5]. The primary goal of therapy in managing MI is to minimize the area of myocardium infarcted [4, 6].

Acute myocardial infarction is one of the clinical syndromes known as acute coronary syndromes (ACS), which include unstable angina, non–ST segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and ST segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) [1, 7, 8].

The most common cause of myocardial ischemia or infarction is the **occlusion of an atherosclerotic coronary artery** [9]. Specifically, Type 1 MI is due to acute occlusion of a severely narrowed atherosclerotic coronary artery by a **coronary thrombus after rupture of an atherosclerotic plaque**, which is the most common cause, occurring in about 90% of patients with acute MI [5, 8]. Other causes include Type 2 MI due to increased [[Oxygen]] demand or decreased delivery (such as from respiratory failure, anemia, hypotension, arrhythmias), Type 3 MI associated with sudden cardiac death from lethal rhythm disturbance, and Types 4 and 5 MI associated with operative procedures [8].

A myocardial infarction at its height typically consists of a central area of dead, necrotic tissue—the **zone of infarction (or necrosis)**—surrounded by a layer of injured myocardial tissue—the **zone of injury**—and then by an outer layer of ischemic tissue—the **zone of ischemia** [10, 11]. MI can be transmural (involving the entire wall thickness from endocardium to epicardium) or nontransmural (involving only part, usually the subendocardial area) [10, 12, 13].

ECG changes in MI include T wave changes indicating myocardial ischemia, ST segment changes indicating myocardial injury, and Q wave changes caused by myocardial necrosis [14, 15]. A pathologic Q wave is produced by irreversible myocardial necrosis after an MI [16].

The term "acute MI" specifically refers to the **initial 24 hours after the onset of an MI** [17].
