# ***Field Reference Pathophysiology***
# Non-Cardiogenic
- **[[COPD 🚬]]**
- **Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)** 
- [[Pneumonia]] & [[LRTI]] -> increased permeability of the alveolar-capillary membrane 
- **Toxic Inhalation**: Exposure to harmful substances, such as smoke or chemical fumes, can damage lung tissue and lead to edema.
- Salt Water Aspiration
- **High Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE)**: Occurs in individuals who ascend to high altitudes too quickly, leading to increased pulmonary artery pressure and fluid leakage.
- **Neurogenic Pulmonary Edema**: Can occur after a severe brain injury or seizure, leading to increased sympathetic nervous system activity and pulmonary vascular permeability.
- Neurogenic APO - Spinal Cord Injury
- **Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury (TRALI)**: A rare but serious reaction to blood transfusions that can cause pulmonary edema.
- **Drug Overdose**: Certain drugs, such as opioids or anesthetics, can lead to respiratory depression and subsequent pulmonary edema.
- **Pancreatitis**: Severe inflammation of the pancreas can lead to systemic inflammatory response and pulmonary edema.
- **Sepsis**: A systemic infection can lead to increased vascular permeability and fluid accumulation in the lungs.
- **Negative Pressure** - Decreased pressure in the alveolus.
- **Hypoxic Pulmonary Vasoconstriction**: Diverting blood flow to well ventilated alveoli. 
# Cardiogenic
- **[[Congestive Heart Failure (CCF)]]** - This is the most common cause, where the heart's pumping ability is compromised, leading to fluid buildup in the lungs.
- **Myocardial Infarction (Heart Attack)**: Damage to the heart muscle can impair its ability to pump blood, resulting in pulmonary edema.
- **Hypertensive Crisis**: Severe hypertension can lead to increased pressure in the heart and lungs, causing fluid to leak into the alveoli.
- **Arrhythmias**: Abnormal heart rhythms can reduce the heart's efficiency, leading to fluid accumulation in the lungs.
- **Valvular Heart Disease**: Conditions affecting the heart valves, such as mitral or aortic valve stenosis or regurgitation, can lead to increased pressure in the heart and pulmonary circulation.
- **Cardiomyopathy**: Diseases of the heart muscle, whether due to genetic factors, alcohol abuse, or other causes, can impair the heart's ability to pump effectively.
- **Pulmonary Embolism**: A blockage in the pulmonary arteries can increase pressure in the right side of the heart, leading to pulmonary edema.
- **Fluid Overload**: Conditions that lead to excessive fluid intake or retention, such as kidney failure, can overwhelm the heart's ability to manage fluid balance.
- **Post-Cardiac Surgery**: Patients may experience cardiogenic pulmonary edema following heart surgery due to changes in heart function or fluid management.
- **Sepsis**: Severe infections can lead to cardiogenic shock, where the heart's ability to pump is severely compromised, resulting in pulmonary edema.
