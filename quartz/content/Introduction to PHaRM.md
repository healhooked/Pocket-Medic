📘 Introduction 

Prehospital and Retrieval Medicine (PHaRM) is the utilization of medical, nursing and paramedic teams to facilitate rapid clinical care and safe transport of a patient from one location to the another. In practice for the past 50 years or so, it has evolved dramatically over the past 20 years and forms the foundation to health care for all communities, in part people who reside in remote, rural and regional areas. 

Historically there has been much debate generated by this field of medical endeavor. The breadth and quality of literature in the field of prehospital and retrieval medicine in general and more specifically in aeromedical retrieval, has continued to flourish. This evidence has strengthened its practice and place in Australian healthcare, not only enabling the ill and injured equal access to specialized care along with improvement and standardized clinical practice across networks.

Much discussion comes down to two basic questions: 

1. Does the practice of prehospital and retrieval medicine provide a benefit to patients? 

2. How does this practice impact on health systems? 

These two questions are not simple and require nuanced considerations, thought and research. 

The discussion about patient benefit is not just a simple measure of clinical outcomes. A simplistic review of outcomes, comparing patients transported with those not transported, may be a valid starting point but gives us relatively meaningless data if the multitude of variables involved in transporting patients aren’t factored. For example, consider the impact of methods of transport, and whether there are extra problems because of flight and exposure of patients to the altered physiology of altitude. In addition, in times of spiraling health costs we need to consider the cost of the provision of prehospital and retrieval medicine, and whether there is an economic benefit. This economic analysis should not just be on the direct comparison of mission costs but also both total system costs and opportunity costs. For example, “Does the deployment of an aircraft mean that ambulance cover is preserved in a regional centre and able to respond to other tasks?” How do you measure this? 

Other variables worthy of consideration is the safety of the mission and the risk to flight crew and patient. This introduces concepts of risk management, as we balance patient outcomes versus crew safety and cost. The answers to these initial questions become even more clouded, the more we analyse the components intrinsic to any retrieval system. It is clear for instance, that not all prehospital and retrieval systems are the same. Not only will there be differences in regional demographics and patient populations but there may be completely different operating platforms and models of care. If one service uses flight nurses and doctors in a fixed wing aircraft over long distances, can we generalize results from this service to a service that transfers shorter distance missions using a rotary wing aircraft staffed by paramedics only? 

Another consideration is patient selection. Appropriate tasking of retrieval teams and assets should consider all these issues so that the best possible outcome for patients is achieved with consideration of the most cost-effective provision of care and ensuring the safety of all parties. Tasking policies may influence outcome analyses. If retrieval assets are only deployed to the most remote and most critical cases, these patients will be self-selected as the most severe often with the longest time to definitive care. As a result of this, is this group more likely to have a poor outcome? 

Similarly, if cases deemed futile only have road assets deployed do we introduce an immediate survival bias in comparison? Direct comparison of patient outcomes between a retrieval service may be misleading in either case. Accurate outcome comparisons would only be possible if the cases were matched, or similar populations compared. And if patient outcomes could be shown to be better in air transport versus road transport what are the key factors that make it so?

The PHaRM system needs to be able to provide answers to these questions as well as providing a service. Outcomes, both clinical and in cost effectiveness need to be consistent, predictable, and able to be described and monitored. This mandates a system-based approach to quality management extending across operational activity, maintenance, and training. This ensures that adverse outcomes are avoided whilst good outcomes are repeated, futility identified, and safety maintained. In addition, reporting structures need to be able to describe this to monitoring agencies and funding bodies to ensure fiscal accountability

Demand for Prehospital and Retrieval Medicine

The requirement of PHRM retrievals continues to grow. These include both prehospital (primary) missions and retrieval (interhospital) missions. 

There are multiple reasons for this, including but not limited to: 

Population growth and ageing
Population distribution to areas with less medical and infrastructure support
Deskilling of the rural and regional medical workforce, particularly post COVID
Centralization of specialized care in larger tertiary centers
Adventure and extreme sports development e.g., BASE jumping, expedition touring, caving, etc.
Many of the readings describe growth in ambulance demand which appears to be an international phenomenon. Coupled with this is the increased demand on Intensive Care (ICU) beds and the increasing centralization of resources. This will only continue the inevitable need for transfer for health care.  With this evidence of increased demand for transport of critical care patients the below reading. 

📖 Reading:  Droogh JM, Smit M, Absalom AR, Ligtenberg JJ, Zijlstra JG. Transferring the critically ill patient: are we there yet? Critical care. 2015 Dec 1;19(1):62.

Link to content: This paper, a narrative reviews, reviews the safety, organization and ongoing challenges associated with interhospital transfers. Essentially whilst interfacility transfers improve access to expert care and reduce mortality, they remain not without risk and are resource intensive. It is this risk that we as retrieval clinicians aim to minimize. 

As you start to understand some of the basic concepts, the following reading below by Thomas et al (2005) highlights enduring controversies in critical care medicine, particularly in trauma and aeromedical retrievals. Despite being over 20 years old, the paper remains highly relevant to the field's ongoing debates.  This excellent review document refers to many acknowledged “landmark” papers in aeromedical retrieval and prehospital care.  After reading this review try and keep the major issues to the fore as we work through the rest of the material in this subject.
